Three elections in one minute

People who chose neither also count

Brazil elects its president in two rounds. If no candidate gets more than half of the valid votes in the first round, the top two meet in a runoff. Official percentages are based on valid votes, which exclude blank and null ballots. Here the count starts from the whole electorate: people who voted for one of the two camps or for another candidate, people who voted blank or null, and people who did not vote at all.

In 2018, Jair Bolsonaro won with {{e2018r2_pct_b_valid}} of the valid vote. In 2022, Lula won with {{e2022r2_pct_l_valid}}. In 2026, Flávio Bolsonaro led the first round with {{e2026r1_pct_b_valid}} to Lula's {{e2026r1_pct_l_valid}}. In the runoff, Lula won with {{e2026r2_pct_l_valid}}.

In every round, between {{absent_min}} and {{absent_max}} of the electorate did not vote. In the 2026 runoff, {{e2026r2_pct_neither_aptos}} of the electorate voted for neither candidate: {{e2026r2_pct_absent_aptos}} did not turn out and {{e2026r2_pct_blank_null_aptos}} voted blank or null.

Voting is compulsory for most Brazilian adults. Abstention figures also include outdated records of people who moved or died, so part of the abstention does not reflect a decision not to vote.

Income, size and region

The vote is also associated with characteristics of the municipalities. The chart shows each group's median and the range where most of its municipalities fall.

Of these three measures, income is the one most closely associated with the vote. In the lowest income band, the median municipality gave Lula {{q7_income1_p50_l}}; in the highest, {{q7_income5_p50_l}}. At the extremes, the results barely overlap. Income and region go together: {{q7_income1_in_ne}} of the {{q7_income1_municipalities}} lowest-income municipalities are in the Northeast, and {{q7_income5_in_s}} of the {{q7_income5_municipalities}} highest-income ones are in the South. Even when each municipality is compared with its region's average, higher income goes with a smaller share for Lula.

There are exceptions on both sides: Flávio won in {{q7_income1_led_by_b}} municipalities of the lowest income band, and Lula in {{q7_income5_led_by_l}} of the highest. And this describes places, not people. High-income municipalities also have low-income voters, and vice versa.

Municipality size is barely associated with the vote in the country as a whole. Small and large municipalities appear on both sides.

2026

A turnaround between two Sundays

In the first round, on October 4, Flávio Bolsonaro finished {{q6_b_lead_r1}} votes ahead of Lula. In the runoff, on October 25, Lula won by {{q6_l_lead_r2}}.

Where other candidates got more votes in the first round, both Lula and Flávio gained more in the runoff. Where turnout rose more, they also gained more. Part of this is arithmetic: in points of the electorate, the two gains add up to the vote for other candidates plus the rise in turnout, minus the change in blank and null votes.

A word of caution. Municipal data do not tell how each person voted. If a municipality had many votes for other candidates and Lula or Flávio gained there, one cannot conclude that those voters switched to that candidate. Drawing that conclusion is known as the ecological fallacy.

    How we did it

    Sources, method and limits

    Sources

    • Votes and turnout: open data portal of Brazil's Superior Electoral Court (TSE), with votes and turnout detail by municipality and electoral zone for 2014, 2018, 2022 and 2026. The 2026 files already include both rounds.
    • Municipality codes: the official TSE table linking TSE codes to IBGE codes, checked against the municipality directory of Base dos Dados, a Brazilian open-data project.
    • Map: IBGE 2025 municipal boundaries, simplified so that shared borders still line up.
    • Population and income: IBGE 2022 Census (tables 4709 and 10295 of SIDRA, IBGE's online database).

    Validation

    Each round's totals, for Brazil, each state and abroad, were compared one by one with the TSE's official results reports and, for 2026, also with the results-portal files. In total, {{validation_cells}} numbers were compared. Only one difference appeared, and it has an explanation: in the 2018 runoff, the municipal file counts voters at polling stations whose votes were not counted as abstention, while the official report lists them separately. For 2014, no official report was available; the check used the section-level vote file, which comes from the same TSE database.

    Decisions

    • Votes cast abroad count in national totals and are left out of the maps.
    • On the share maps, color shows each camp's share of the two camps' combined votes. In first rounds, this leaves out the other candidates.
    • On the dot map, each dot is worth {{dot_votes}} votes and is placed at a random spot inside its municipality, with a fixed seed. The position inside the municipality has no meaning.
    • Boa Esperança do Norte (MT) was formally established in 2025, with parts of Sorriso and Nova Ubiratã, and had its first presidential election in 2026. The 2026 results of those two municipalities cover a smaller area than in earlier elections.
    • “Did not vote” is the eligible electorate minus turnout. It includes voters at polling stations that did not open.

    Limits

    • Municipal data describe places, not people. No number here says how any individual voted.
    • The associations with income, size, third candidates and turnout do not indicate cause.
    • The comparison with 2014 measures the candidate who faced the PT in the runoff, not the same political group.
    • Abstention includes outdated records of people who moved or died.
