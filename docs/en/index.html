Brazil is not binary

The map of who won each city shows a country split in two. The votes show something else: every municipality has voters on both sides, plus people who backed another candidate, cast a blank or null vote, or stayed home.

The question

Is Brazil divided?

In the 2026 runoff, Lula won in 2,875 municipalities and Flávio Bolsonaro in 2,695. Counted that way, the municipalities seem to form two countries of almost the same size, each in its own color.

46.6 million

people voted, in the 2026 runoff, for the candidate who lost in their own municipality. That is 37.6% of the valid votes cast in Brazil.

This story uses official data from the latest presidential elections to show that each side's vote is spread across the whole country. It also shows where it is not: in some regions and income bands one side dominates, and the story gives that the same prominence.

How we compare different elections. Votes are grouped into two camps. Lula camp: Fernando Haddad in 2018 and Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in 2022 and 2026. Bolsonaro camp: Jair Bolsonaro in 2018 and 2022 and his son Flávio Bolsonaro in 2026. The 2014 election, between Dilma Rousseff and Aécio Neves, appears only as a baseline, in the same colors and always with the candidates' names; neither is counted in either camp. Municipality names are followed by their state's two-letter abbreviation.

Three elections in one minute

People who chose neither also count

Brazil elects its president in two rounds. If no candidate gets more than half of the valid votes in the first round, the top two meet in a runoff. Official percentages are based on valid votes, which exclude blank and null ballots. Here the count starts from the whole electorate: people who voted for one of the two camps or for another candidate, people who voted blank or null, and people who did not vote at all.

In 2018, Jair Bolsonaro won with 55.1% of the valid vote. In 2022, Lula won with 50.9%. In 2026, Flávio Bolsonaro led the first round with 47.0% to Lula's 45.2%. In the runoff, Lula won with 51.8%.

The Lula camp's highest share was 40.5% of the electorate, reached by Lula in the 2026 runoff. The Bolsonaro camp's highest was 39.2%, reached by Jair Bolsonaro in the 2018 runoff. In every round, between 19.2% and 21.3% of the electorate did not vote. In the 2026 runoff, 21.8% of the electorate voted for neither candidate: 19.2% did not turn out and 2.6% voted blank or null.

Voting is compulsory for most Brazilian adults. Abstention figures also include outdated records of people who moved or died, so part of the abstention does not reflect a decision not to vote.

The map that misleads

A country in two colors, or in many

This is the usual map. Each municipality takes the color of whoever got the most votes there. In the 2026 runoff, Lula won in 2,875 municipalities and Flávio in 2,695.

The municipalities Lula won hold half of the electorate registered in Brazil, and those Flávio won hold the other half (50.0% and 50.0%). It looks like a country cut in half. But the color of each municipality hides everyone who voted for the other candidate there.

Now the same election as shares. The closer to pink, the more balanced the municipality. In 3,554 municipalities, the candidate who lost locally got more than 30% of the vote. In 1,804, more than 40%.

Finally, each dot is worth 1,000 votes and sits at a random spot inside its municipality. The map now shows where the votes are. Where more voters live, the two colors mix.

Lula got 24.3 million votes in municipalities Flávio won. Flávio got 22.3 million in municipalities Lula won. That is 37.9% and 37.4% of each candidate's votes in Brazil.

Nobody lives in a bubble

The side that loses a city does not vanish from it

In the 2026 runoff, the losing side got more than 10% of the valid vote in 5,558 of the 5,571 municipalities.

Flávio got 10.8 million votes in the Northeast, more than in 11 states of other regions combined, with the Federal District counted as a state (Amapá, Roraima, Acre, Tocantins, Rondônia, Mato Grosso do Sul, Amazonas, Federal District, Espírito Santo, Mato Grosso and Pará). Lula got 6.8 million in the South, more than in 11 states of other regions combined (Roraima, Acre, Amapá, Rondônia, Tocantins, Mato Grosso do Sul, Mato Grosso, Federal District, Sergipe, Espírito Santo and Amazonas). In the North, the two were 1,079 votes apart, out of 10.0 million votes.

That does not mean regions vote alike. In the Northeast, Lula won in 1,774 of the 1,794 municipalities. In the South, Flávio won in 1,054 of 1,191, and in the Center-West in 406 of 468. Lula's lead in the Northeast was 13.7 million votes. Flávio's, adding up the Southeast, South and Center-West, was 9.3 million.

Among municipalities with 100,000 voters or more, the most pro-Lula was Cametá (PA), where Flávio got 21.8%. The most pro-Bolsonaro was Sinop (MT), where Lula got 22.3%.

Income, size and region

The vote is also associated with characteristics of the municipalities. The chart shows each group's median and the range where most of its municipalities fall.

Of these three measures, income is the one most closely associated with the vote. In the lowest income band, the median municipality gave Lula 76.4%; in the highest, 31.9%. At the extremes, the results barely overlap. Income and region go together: 907 of the 1,114 lowest-income municipalities are in the Northeast, and 578 of the 836 highest-income ones are in the South. Even when each municipality is compared with its region's average, higher income goes with a smaller share for Lula.

There are exceptions on both sides: Flávio won in 32 municipalities of the lowest income band, and Lula in 24 of the highest. And this describes places, not people. High-income municipalities also have low-income voters, and vice versa.

Municipality size is barely associated with the vote in the country as a whole. Small and large municipalities appear on both sides.

2018

The PT's opponent gains ground in all five regions

To measure Jair Bolsonaro's rise, the comparison starts from the 2014 runoff, when Aécio Neves of the Brazilian Social Democracy Party (PSDB) faced Dilma Rousseff of the Workers' Party (PT). It is not the same political movement. What is measured is how much the vote share of the PT's runoff opponent grew, municipality by municipality.

The share of the PT's opponent rose by 8.4 points in the North, 9.2 points in the Southeast, 9.4 points in the South and 9.1 points in the Center-West. In the Northeast, it rose 2.0 points. In 775 municipalities, Dilma had won in 2014 and Bolsonaro won in 2018. In 58, it went the other way: Aécio won in 2014 and Haddad in 2018.

Among municipalities with 50,000 voters or more, the 10 largest increases were all in Rio de Janeiro state. The largest was in Belford Roxo (RJ). There, Aécio got 25.2% of the valid vote in 2014, and Bolsonaro 68.9% in 2018.

Nationwide, Bolsonaro got 6.8 million more runoff votes than Aécio. Haddad got 7.5 million fewer than Dilma.

2022

Lula's return runs through the Southeast

From 2018 to 2022, Lula got 13.3 million more runoff votes than Haddad. Bolsonaro got 409,000 more than in the previous election.

The Lula camp's share rose by 11.1 points in the Southeast, 6.4 points in the South, 6.3 points in the Center-West and 0.9 points in the North. In the Northeast it barely moved (−0.3 points). The Southeast accounted for 58.9% of the Lula camp's vote gain inside Brazil.

In all, 368 municipalities went from Bolsonaro to Lula, and 54 went the other way. Among municipalities with 50,000 voters or more, the 10 largest increases were all in São Paulo state. The largest was in Cruzeiro (SP): 17.0 points.

2026

A turnaround between two Sundays

In the first round, on October 4, Flávio Bolsonaro finished 2.2 million votes ahead of Lula. In the runoff, on October 25, Lula won by 4.4 million.

Where other candidates got more votes in the first round, both Lula and Flávio gained more in the runoff. Where turnout rose more, they also gained more. Part of this is arithmetic: in points of the electorate, the two gains add up to the vote for other candidates plus the rise in turnout, minus the change in blank and null votes.

Across the ten groups in the table (municipalities split into fifths by the vote for other candidates and by the change in turnout), Lula gained more than Flávio in every one, by 3.7 to 5.1 points of the electorate. Municipality by municipality, Lula gained more than Flávio in 5,230; Flávio gained more than Lula in 340.

In 211 municipalities, Flávio was ahead in the first round and Lula finished ahead in the runoff. In one, Uirapuru (GO), it went the other way.

Among municipalities with 50,000 voters or more, Lula's largest gain was in Mariana (MG): 11.2 points of the electorate. Flávio's was in Trindade (GO): 7.6 points. In absolute votes, both candidates' largest gains came in the same municipality, São Paulo (SP): 812,000 for Lula and 337,000 for Flávio.

A word of caution. Municipal data do not tell how each person voted. If a municipality had many votes for other candidates and Lula or Flávio gained there, one cannot conclude that those voters switched to that candidate. Drawing that conclusion is known as the ecological fallacy.

    How we did it

    Sources, method and limits

    Sources

    • Votes and turnout: open data portal of Brazil's Superior Electoral Court (TSE), with votes and turnout detail by municipality and electoral zone for 2014, 2018, 2022 and 2026. The 2026 files already include both rounds.
    • Municipality codes: the official TSE table linking TSE codes to IBGE codes, checked against the municipality directory of Base dos Dados, a Brazilian open-data project.
    • Map: IBGE 2025 municipal boundaries, simplified so that shared borders still line up.
    • Population and income: IBGE 2022 Census (tables 4709 and 10295 of SIDRA, IBGE's online database).

    Validation

    Each round's totals, for Brazil, each state and abroad, were compared one by one with the TSE's official results reports and, for 2026, also with the results-portal files. In total, 3,625 numbers were compared. Only one difference appeared, and it has an explanation: in the 2018 runoff, the municipal file counts voters at polling stations whose votes were not counted as abstention, while the official report lists them separately. For 2014, no official report was available; the check used the section-level vote file, which comes from the same TSE database.

    Decisions

    • Votes cast abroad count in national totals and are left out of the maps.
    • On the share maps, color shows each camp's share of the two camps' combined votes. In first rounds, this leaves out the other candidates.
    • On the dot map, each dot is worth 1,000 votes and is placed at a random spot inside its municipality, with a fixed seed. The position inside the municipality has no meaning.
    • Boa Esperança do Norte (MT) was formally established in 2025, with parts of Sorriso and Nova Ubiratã, and had its first presidential election in 2026. The 2026 results of those two municipalities cover a smaller area than in earlier elections.
    • “Did not vote” is the eligible electorate minus turnout. It includes voters at polling stations that did not open.

    Limits

    • Municipal data describe places, not people. No number here says how any individual voted.
    • The associations with income, size, third candidates and turnout do not indicate cause.
    • The comparison with 2014 measures the candidate who faced the PT in the runoff, not the same political group.
    • Abstention includes outdated records of people who moved or died.
